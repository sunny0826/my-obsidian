# Laws of Software Engineering
A collection of principles and patterns that shape software systems, teams, and decisions.
Source: https://lawsofsoftwareengineering.com/

## 56 Laws by Category

### Architecture
1. Conway's Law — Organizations design systems that mirror their own communication structure.
2. Hyrum's Law — With a sufficient number of API users, all observable behaviors of your system will be depended on by somebody.
3. Gall's Law — A complex system that works is invariably found to have evolved from a simple system that worked.
4. The Law of Leaky Abstractions — All non-trivial abstractions, to some degree, are leaky.
5. Tesler's Law (Conservation of Complexity) — Every application has an inherent amount of irreducible complexity that can only be shifted, not eliminated.
6. CAP Theorem — A distributed system can guarantee only two of: consistency, availability, and partition tolerance.
7. Second-System Effect — Small, successful systems tend to be followed by overengineered, bloated replacements.
8. Fallacies of Distributed Computing — A set of eight false assumptions that new distributed system designers often make.
9. Law of Unintended Consequences — Whenever you change a complex system, expect surprise.
10. Zawinski's Law — Every program attempts to expand until it can read mail.

### Teams
11. Conway's Law — Organizations design systems that mirror their own communication structure.
12. Brooks's Law — Adding manpower to a late software project makes it later.
13. Dunbar's Number — There is a cognitive limit of about 150 stable relationships one person can maintain.
14. The Ringelmann Effect — Individual productivity decreases as group size increases.
15. Price's Law — The square root of the total number of participants does 50% of the work.
16. Putt's Law — Those who understand technology don't manage it, and those who manage it don't understand it.
17. Peter Principle — In a hierarchy, every employee tends to rise to their level of incompetence.
18. Bus Factor — The minimum number of team members whose loss would put the project in serious trouble.
19. Dilbert Principle — Companies tend to promote incompetent employees to management to limit the damage they can do.

### Planning
20. Premature Optimization (Knuth's Optimization Principle) — Premature optimization is the root of all evil.
21. Parkinson's Law — Work expands to fill the time available for its completion.
22. The Ninety-Ninety Rule — The first 90% of the code accounts for the first 90% of development time; the remaining 10% accounts for the other 90%.
23. Hofstadter's Law — It always takes longer than you expect, even when you take into account Hofstadter's Law.
24. Goodhart's Law — When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.
25. Gilb's Law — Anything you need to quantify can be measured in some way better than not measuring it.

### Quality
26. The Boy Scout Rule — Leave the code better than you found it.
27. YAGNI (You Aren't Gonna Need It) — Don't add functionality until it is necessary.
28. Murphy's Law / Sod's Law — Anything that can go wrong will go wrong.
29. Postel's Law — Be conservative in what you do, be liberal in what you accept from others.
30. Broken Windows Theory — Don't leave broken windows (bad designs, wrong decisions, or poor code) unrepaired.
31. Technical Debt — Technical Debt is everything that slows us down when developing software.
32. Linus's Law — Given enough eyeballs, all bugs are shallow.
33. Kernighan's Law — Debugging is twice as hard as writing the code in the first place.
34. Testing Pyramid — A project should have many fast unit tests, fewer integration tests, and only a small number of UI tests.
35. Pesticide Paradox — Repeatedly running the same tests becomes less effective over time.
36. Lehman'x's Laws of Software Evolution — Software that reflects the real world must evolve, and that evolution has predictable limits.
37. Sturgeon's Law — 90% of everything is crap.

### Scale
38. Amdahl's Law — The speedup from parallelization is limited by the fraction of work that cannot be parallelized.
39. Gustafson's Law — It is possible to achieve significant speedup in parallel processing by increasing the problem size.
40. Metcalfe's Law — The value of a network is proportional to the square of the number of users.

### Design
41. DRY (Don't Repeat Yourself) — Every piece of knowledge must have a single, unambiguous, authoritative representation.
42. KISS (Keep It Simple, Stupid) — Designs and systems should be as simple as possible.
43. SOLID Principles — Five main guidelines that enhance software design, making code more maintainable and scalable.
44. Law of Demeter — An object should only interact with its immediate friends, not strangers.
45. Principle of Least Astonishment — Software and interfaces should behave in a way that least surprises users and other developers.

### Decisions
46. Dunning-Kruger Effect — The less you know about something, the more confident you tend to be.
47. Hanlon's Razor — Never attribute to malice that which is adequately explained by stupidity or carelessness.
48. Occam's Razor — The simplest explanation is often the most accurate one.
49. Sunk Cost Fallacy — Sticking with a choice because you've invested time or energy in it, even when walking away helps you.
50. The Map Is Not the Territory — Our representations of reality are not the same as reality itself.
51. Confirmation Bias — A tendency to favor information that supports our existing beliefs or ideas.
52. The Hype Cycle & Amara's Law — We tend to overestimate the effect of a technology in the short run and underestimate the impact in the long run.
53. The Lindy Effect — The longer something has been in use, the more likely it is to continue being used.
54. First Principles Thinking — Breaking a complex problem into its most basic blocks and then building up from there.
55. Inversion — Solving a problem by considering the opposite outcome and working backward from it.
56. Pareto Principle (80/20 Rule) — 80% of the problems result from 20% of the causes.
57. Cunningham's Law — The best way to get the correct answer on the Internet is not to ask a question, it's to post the wrong answer.
